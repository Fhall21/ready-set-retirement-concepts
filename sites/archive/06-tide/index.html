A tide is not a scare story. It is a schedule.

Tsunami is the wrong word, and the wrong word makes it easy to ignore. A tsunami arrives without notice. This one has been on the books since the nineteen-fifties. Every person it concerns is on your payroll right now, with a birthday and a date in mind.

Which means it can be planned for, and almost nowhere is. Organisations model the superannuation, the payout, the succession chart and the handover checklist. What they do not model is the person walking out, and what happens to them in the eighteen months after the farewell cake.

Mastery leaves quietly.

The handover document captures the tasks. It does not capture the judgement. Who to ring when the system is down at four on a Friday. Which supplier will bend and which one will not. Why the process has that odd step in it, and what went wrong the year somebody removed it.

That knowledge was never written down because it was never separable from the person. It leaves in a car park at the end of a Friday, and the organisation only discovers what it was worth when it has to buy the answer back from a consultant.

At riskRecruitment cost is the visible line. The invisible lines are the eleven months of reduced output while a capable replacement learns what could not be handed over, and the two or three colleagues who quietly start looking because the person who made the job bearable has gone.

Meanwhile the leaver is having their own bad year. Loss of direction, identity, purpose and social connection are the recognised dark side of retirement, and they arrive on a schedule as reliable as the tide. People who fall into it come back — as contractors, as re-hires, as very expensive lessons — or they do not come back at all.

Where your cohorts actually sit, and what each one needs. The useful work happens in the shallows. Almost all of the spending happens at the bottom.

Still building, still ambitious, and entirely willing to think about it because it is not yet personal. This is the cheapest and most effective place to intervene, and the one nobody uses.

05Years out

Decisions are being made privately and told to nobody at work. Deferred projects, declined promotions, a quiet withdrawal from anything with a three-year horizon. Most employers read this as coasting.

02Years out

The window for a real handover, and for the leaver to work out what replaces the structure, the status and the people. Two years is enough. It is rarely offered.

At riskDiscretionary effort goes first, and it goes without comment. The organisation is planning a morning tea. The employee is planning the rest of their life with no method for doing it.

00Year one, after

At riskMonday with nowhere to be. Friendships that turned out to be structural rather than chosen. Being introduced without a job title. This is where the damage is done, and it lands months after your duty of care is assumed to have ended.

Shallow is preparation. Deep is repair.

The water does not stop. The vessel changes.

Nothing an employer does will slow the tide. What can be changed is what your people are sitting in when it lifts them. A hull with a keel under it holds a heading in weather that capsizes a flat-bottomed boat. Preparation is the keel.
