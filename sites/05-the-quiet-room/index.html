What nobody puts in the speech

Here it is in plain words. If none of this is you, close the page with our blessing. If some of it is, keep going. It is far more common than the silence around it would suggest.

Loss of direction, identity, purpose and connection. Recognised in the research on retirement. Rarely mentioned at the farewell.

The first Monday felt like a holiday. So did the second. By the fourth you are awake at ten past six regardless, because forty years of it does not switch off on request, and by half past seven you are dressed and fed and standing in the kitchen with the whole day in front of you and no reason to begin it at any particular hour. The days stop having edges. Nothing arrives to tell you that one of them has finished.

Structure is the first thing to go and the last thing people think to replace.

You thought of them as friends, and most of them were, in the way that people you sit near for eleven years are friends. But the sitting near was doing the work. The friendship was held up by the building, the corridor, the ten minutes before a meeting started. Take the scaffolding away and what is left is the effort of arranging things, and that effort falls to whoever wants it more. You find out fairly quickly that it was rarely you. Two of them stayed. That is not a betrayal. It is arithmetic, and nobody warned you about it.

Structural friendship, not chosen friendship. The distinction only becomes visible after the structure has gone.

At somebody's sixtieth a man you have not met asks what you do. You hear yourself begin with I was, name a job you no longer hold, and then, because the sentence has nowhere left to go, add so, retired now. He nods. He has run out of questions and so have you. Driving home you work out that you have just described yourself to a stranger entirely in the past tense, that you had no second sentence ready, and that you have not had one since March.

None of that is necessarily depression. Some of it is, and that is worth saying to a doctor. Most of it is something with less of a name, which is exactly why it goes unattended for a year.

Forty years of being introduced by a job title, and then one evening the title is in the past tense and the sentence has no second half.

A table, a notebook, a Tuesday morning that has been given a shape. Almost none of the good version of this happens by accident. Somebody decided it would.

None of this means the decision was wrong. It means it was unaccompanied.

We spend forty years planning for retirement financially and roughly ten minutes planning who we will be when it arrives. The money question gets a professional, a strategy, an annual review and a spreadsheet that runs to age ninety. The other question gets a good luck and a card.

That imbalance is not a character flaw. It is what happens when one half of a problem has an entire industry attached to it and the other half has almost nobody.

Money funds a lifestyle. It cannot buy a quality of life, and it has never once told anyone what to do with a Wednesday.

Financial planning answers what you will live on. This answers what you will live for. The yin to financial planning's yang, and not a substitute for either.

The work itself is unglamorous, and it holds.

Dr Sandra Walden-Pearson works on the human side of the change. She calls it human-centred retirement planning™, and the method she teaches is readysetretirement psychosocial planning®: deliberate preparation for identity, purpose, structure and belonging. Done in advance where that is still possible, and done retrospectively where it is not, which is most of the time and perfectly workable.

In practice it looks like conversations with homework. You map what your work was actually giving you, which is almost never what you would have guessed, and then you go about sourcing those same things from somewhere that is not a payroll. Some of it is straightforward. Some of it takes a year. It is closer to rehabilitation than to inspiration, and it is deliberately slow, because the things it rebuilds are slow things.

It runs as one-to-one coaching, group coaching, workshops, seminars, training, consulting and speaking, depending on whether you are doing this on your own or an organisation is doing it for two hundred people at once.

She holds a PhD in business and an MBA in human resources, and is a certified professional retirement coach, a certified resilience coach and an accredited mental health first aider. That is the short version and it is enough of it.

Works with individuals across Australia, and with organisations planning for a workforce that is retiring faster than it ever has.
